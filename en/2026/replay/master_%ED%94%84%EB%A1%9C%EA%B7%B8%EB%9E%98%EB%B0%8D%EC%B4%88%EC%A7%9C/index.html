Written by: Kera

Back in high school, I — the person writing this — once took on NYPC. That was right around the time I was first becoming interested in problem solving, and what left a strong impression on me, unlike other contests, was that the problems genuinely mixed in elements from all sorts of games, and that the format asked you to find your own optimized answer among many possible ones. I couldn't really participate properly at the time, since my skills were lacking and I had to juggle schoolwork, but I still have memories of solving and discussing problems together with my high school friends, and I even attended last year's 101010th anniversary offline event — so this contest has always meant a lot to me.

Time passed, I graduated from high school, and I thought my connection with NYPC was over. But then I heard that the NYPC University Code Battle, which had felt like a trial run last year, was being launched officially under the name NYPC Master Track — so I resolved that this time I would take part properly. And so I decided to take it on together with bananasoyami and GGRIM. Both teammates have excellent mathematical reasoning and are skilled at strategic design, so I felt very reassured.

After registering, we played the mushroom game ourselves for a bit, talked it over, and discussed a little about how we should approach things once the actual qualification round began. Then we spent the time until the qualification round on final exams and personal activities. Time passed, and the day the qualification round began arrived.

On June 29, when I read the problem that had just been made public, two thoughts came into my head: "this is quite complex" and "this is going to be quite fun."

Reading through the game rulebook calmly, it looked like it was built on top of "Civilization" and "StarCraft." But between the 200200200-turn limit, the order of actions, and the detailed movement rules and battlefield generation rules — the fact that it explained even the basic parts of the game in such depth gave me a feeling that this was going to get fairly complicated.

So, in order to understand the rules that weren't clicking for me and to work out a strategy, I first built a simulator I could play myself. In the case of the mushroom game — the practice game — you could play it in the simulator by default, which made understanding the rules and devising strategy really convenient. However, right after building it myself, what I felt was: "ah, now I see why they didn't make one for us this time."

The more soldiers there were each turn, and the more complex the operation I wanted to carry out, the more commands I actually had to issue — and since this had to go on for close to 200200200 turns, it was obvious that playing a single game properly would take an enormous amount of time.

So I gave up on devising strategy by playing it myself, and instead started by looking at the sample code they provided. The sample code simply had logic that sent 333 warriors marching forward right at the start, and beyond learning how to write the code itself, it didn't look like there was anything to gain from it.

The next day, June 30, was the day I decided I should lay the groundwork for the actual working environment going forward, based on the rules and sample code I had checked the day before. The previous day I had used an LLM to write a few pieces of code that came to mind, but it didn't produce results as satisfying as I'd expected. So, to fix that, I thought hard about more efficient ways of instructing it and of writing the code.

(As for the LLM models used, we worked on the basis of Anthropic's Claude, mainly using Sonnet and Fable.)

Here's what I realized:

  1. Leaving the entire code-writing process to the LLM is not a good approach. So we need to structure things well enough that we can read, at least roughly at the code level, how the actual logic works.
  2. For the strategy design process, you definitely should not rely on the LLM's help. At first we discussed with it what the best strategy would be based on the rules, but as time went on I realized that the strategies we thought through and built ourselves were far more efficient and far better.
  3. When writing prompts, organize what you want implemented as cleanly as possible before handing it over. In particular, numbering your requirements one by one (item 1 ~, item 2 ~) rather than writing in conversational prose produces much better results.

Based on what we'd realized, we split the code's structure into a variable declaration section, a section that reads basic server information, and the decide() function where the actual strategy lives — and once we wrote the code that way, we were thankfully able to implement the strategy we wanted.

And so on July 2, with submission 46329, we managed to produce a strategy that reached Gold tier1 for the first time. To describe it a little more precisely, the basic skeleton of this strategy was as follows.

  1. Capture the center point of the map and its surroundings as the top priority, and after that simply capture the strongholds near our HQ one by one.
  2. Once capturing is finished, dispatch 5 warriors at a time to enemy strongholds to destroy them, build a stronghold on that spot, and expand one by one.
  3. Level up the HQ to level 3 along the way, and after that watch the opponent and stay +1+1+1 above them.

There were minor HQ defense routines and other details mixed in, but overall this was the concept of our first strategy.

However, even after going through numerous error fixes and logic revisions via matches against the sample AI, fighting actual opponents in the Pretest turned out to be no easy matter. The problems I wrote down in my notes at the time were as follows.

On July 3–4, I spent time further analyzing and shoring up the problems with the current strategy.

First, to solve two problems — the very early build-up issue and the pointless attacks that kept going even when the opponent's defense was holding — I built logic that could track my own gold accurately. This let me construct bases more precisely in line with the money I actually had.

And building on this gold-tracking logic, I tried to revise the attack strategy so that we would also track the opponent's money and attack when they couldn't respond… but it wasn't easy.

Fundamentally, the biggest issue was that the code as a whole kept breaking during the writing process. Whenever I implemented one thing, features that used to work fine kept falling apart, so when experimenting via matches against the sample AI, I'd see a situation where I beat 5 of them and lost to the 6th flip to the exact opposite once I revised the code — which was rather demoralizing. The root cause of this turned out to be that, as the code gradually got longer and more complex, I could no longer smoothly follow the code the LLM had written.

Still, by somehow pouring in the hours and resolving the problems one by one:

we got to knock on the door of Diamond2, which had looked impossibly far away at first. Just two days earlier, my teammates and I would be trembling whenever we ran into the occasional Diamond in matchmaking… but in the end, with all of us working hard together, we got there.

Having achieved results we were reasonably satisfied with at the time, I think everyone started to find it more and more fun. And so our daily rhythm came to revolve around the Pretest submission times of 101010 AM3, 333 PM, and 999 PM, taking turns sleeping in shifts to line up our schedules — we really threw ourselves into that last week (I think I may have worked harder than during university exam season).

However, even though we resolved various existing problems, I felt a great deal of frustration.

No matter how many problems we fixed, new errors and problems kept appearing, and as our tier rose I got the feeling that it would be difficult to aim any higher on the basis of our current strategy.

So, among several strategic ideas, we decided to reimplement from scratch the one we expected to be most effective: a strategy of attacking at the very earliest stage to widen the resource gap between the opponent and us. The so-called "all-out rush" strategy. The basis for choosing this strategy was the thought that opponents wouldn't be able to respond well to an attack early on when they had no resources, and the fact that when we met opponents who actually ran it, we got absolutely wrecked without room to breathe. So I wanted to design it myself and draw some inspiration from it.

And so after the 3 PM submission on July 5, I designed all of the code from scratch myself and built this all-out rush strategy.

On July 6, after grinding myself through an all-nighter, I finally finished it! The initial implementation of the all-out rush strategy was as follows.

  1. Of the first 3 warriors, send 1 to the stronghold nearest our HQ, and have the remaining 1 take the base on our side that is nearest the map's central stronghold.

  2. After taking 2 strongholds, designate the stronghold nearest the enemy's stronghold as the "rally point."

  3. After that, train troops and gather them at the rally point; once 5 or more warriors beyond the minimum labor force have gathered, invade the nearest stronghold 5 at a time. The key point is that, unlike before, we don't build a stronghold — we immediately use those troops to press on and invade the enemy's strongholds.

  4. Once all strongholds are captured, gather troops after that and march them into the enemy HQ to finish the game.

  5. Throughout this entire process, as long as the HQ is not in danger, the minimum labor force for each base and the HQ must always remain in place.

The finished all-out rush strategy clearly performed better than the previous one. The biggest point in particular: with the previous strategy,

we often lost without being able to lay a finger on opponents even slightly stronger than us, whereas this strategy

brought results good enough that mid-Diamond was well within reach. Of course, Ruby tier4 was still out of our league.

And so we kept discussing with the team, divided the working hours among ourselves, and kept revising things one by one. There were 6 versions in particular that became the backbone, and those are the files you can see in my VS Code below.

The first.py file you can see above is the file that, through the all-out rush strategy, first won 888 matches against the sample AI. Honestly… at first I wondered whether we could ever beat sample AI strategy number 8, so I was really happy.

After that we fought actual opponents and went through various strategic revisions.

First, the biggest problem was the number of strongholds built in the all-out rush strategy. The initial all-out rush strategy I described above builds only 222 strongholds. As a result, as soon as the map got even a little larger, the efficiency of attacks starting from those strongholds dropped sharply, and we simply could not win against an opponent who just built up.

So we simply set the number of bases to build to (number of buildable strongholds on my side/3)(\text{number of buildable strongholds on my side} / 3)(number of buildable strongholds on my side/3), which produced better results than before… but depending on the situation it was still sometimes too few or too many, so problems remained.

And although we went through many revisions — calculation formulas, adding logic that decides based on the shape of the map, and so on — in the end we never fully solved it.

Since we still had no optimization for base construction or movement based on the shape of the map, we also confirmed that when we ran into a similar all-out rush strategy, we would lose to teams that had optimized better than us. So in the early hours of July 7, to fix this, we added logic that identifies when the opponent is running an all-out rush and commits to defense. The conditions for identifying an opponent as running an all-out rush are as follows.

  1. The opponent maintains 222 bases for 202020 or more turns.
  2. After that, they do not build or upgrade bases and spend all their money on training warriors.

And the process carried out once an all-out rush is identified is as follows.

  1. Terminate all attack processes being carried out.
  2. Always maintain 222 bases, and if one is destroyed, rebuild it at the buildable location nearest the HQ.
  3. Always keep the total warrior count at 777 or more.
  4. For every base, if an enemy warrior comes within 444 tiles, add it to the "watch" list.
  5. If, for a base on the "watch" list, an enemy warrior is detected within 333 tiles on the next turn, send 333 warriors to that base.
  6. If a base is under direct attack, keep sending warriors continuously.
  7. If a base is removed from the "watch" list, gather all available soldiers at that location (warriors beyond the minimum force) back to the HQ.
  8. If enough money accumulates to upgrade the HQ, upgrade it.

Through the above process we were able to win most matchups against all-out rush strategies. In the 10 AM simulation on July 7, we confirmed that when attacking, if the opponent had a level 222 HQ, we could not break through even by attacking with 555 warriors. So we revised the code that sent a base of 555 to send 5+2×(opponent’s level−1)5+2\times(\text{opponent's level}-1)5+2×(opponent’s level−1) instead. Using this code we were able to put up our best result.

After the all-out rush strategy, the biggest upgrade was building a system that tracks the opponent's movement. The idea started from the basic fact that movement paths are predetermined. Namely, that movement from a given starting point to a destination always follows the path of minimum Euclidean sum.

But while trying to understand the battlefield generation rules in order to understand these movement rules more precisely, I realized one extremely important fact. Namely, that moving by minimum Euclidean sum does not necessarily guarantee the minimum number of tiles. In particular, I learned that in the method of constructing the map using Delaunay triangulation, the large polygons that form on the outside create many routes that take few tiles but have a long Euclidean distance. I was able to properly grasp this thanks to my teammates GGRIM and bananasoyami, who have strong mathematical reasoning.

In any case, on the basis of this movement scheme, we built logic that predicts enemy troop movement: assuming the opponent is moving toward their own or our HQ and strongholds, it investigates every possible minimum-Euclidean-sum path and then eliminates candidates one by one as they deviate from those paths. It was basically put together by blending various exhaustive search algorithms including BFS, and thankfully it didn't hit the time limit.

Another one of the big upgrades was building a combat simulation algorithm. To solve a problem that had been chronic even in the strategies before the all-out rush — attacking and continually losing troops even when we couldn't actually destroy the enemy's bases and HQ — we needed a simulation that calculated when we could actually destroy an enemy base.

So we built a simulation that, according to the given combat rules, calculates the actual minimum number of troops needed to destroy a base given the base's level and defending troops, taking both our HQ level and the opponent's into account.

Just in case we hit the time limit, we even prepared a dataset covering every case ourselves… but it never really ended up being needed.

At any rate, through this we completed a strategy upgraded beyond the previous one, predicting the opponent's movements to avoid areas of strength while digging into their weak points, and

although it was only the Pretest standings, we got to visit Ruby briefly — it was really… a lot of fun.

But that was short-lived. Users who defended against this style of early attack gradually increased, and as we moved up into higher-ranked strategies, defense processes were established as a matter of course — so our ranking started to fall.

And so on the night of July 7, with one day left until the final submission, we held an emergency meeting with the team. A full 666-hour meeting… through which we came to realize a great many things.

First, that this game is nothing more than rock-paper-scissors. The 333 most fundamental strategies that govern this game — attack, defense, and build-up — beat and lose to each other in a rock-paper-scissors relationship. So the all-out rush strategy had a hard time beating defense strategies, whereas the build-up strategy we implemented first beat defense strategies easily.

After realizing this, we had 222 options.

  1. Develop the all-out rush strategy we'd been running even further.
  2. Build an entirely new strategy that appropriately mixes defense and build-up.

And we decided to just do both.

bananasoyami, who among the team had thought the most about the all-out rush strategy and was mainly handling the related work, would develop the existing strategy further, while GGRIM and I would design a new strategy completely from scratch together. And so, with our sleep patterns already thoroughly wrecked from running the contest, we pulled another all-nighter.

The foundation of the new strategy was to mix defense and build-up appropriately. The reason lay in the nature of defense strategies. A defense strategy collectively refers to making your bases unbreakable by the opponent's attacks, and we discovered that by exploiting the fact that the attacker normally spends more time moving, it isn't too late to respond after watching the opponent's movements.

In other words, defense is relatively lower-difficulty than attacking. (Of course, with more advanced strategies this changes once you use things like fake movement… but we set that aside for now.)

So we set the basic concept as a strategy that defends against all of the opponent's attacks while gradually growing our own bases to aim for a health win, and carried out the detailed implementation as follows.

  1. The basic action principle is to prioritize building bases starting from the buildable stronghold area nearest our HQ. All soldier dispatches happen when a base can be built as soon as they arrive at the construction area.

  2. The game's basic premise is that every stronghold and the HQ always has warriors stationed equal to the minimum labor force.

  3. All movement basically moves along the route that minimizes the number of "tiles" between origin and destination. However, since by the movement rules movement proceeds in the direction that minimizes the sum of Euclidean distances, if the minimum-tile movement differs from the path minimizing the Euclidean distance sum, then split the destination into 222, 333… parts if necessary so that movement always takes the minimum number of tiles. However, split the destination as few times as possible.

  4. If there are multiple routes taking the minimum number of tiles, preferentially choose the route that passes through one of our bases along the way where possible.

  5. If, once a warrior's route to its destination has been decided by rules 3 and 4, the route passes through one of our strongholds, use the "switching" technique to move.

    • Switching: the switching technique is a strategy for moving faster by using another base that lies between the destination and the starting point.

      The way to execute it: if the warrior's location is the tile immediately before the base it passes through, then at the same time as the warrior moves into that base, move 111 warrior that was in the base (one that was filling the minimum labor force, etc.) onward along the route the original warrior was taking. This makes it possible to gain 111 turn.

      That is, when a situation arises where the switching technique can be used, note that the warrior's movement should target the intermediate stronghold rather than aiming at the destination from the start.

  6. When training and dispatching warriors from the HQ, just as with the switching technique, move the personnel already there and train at the same time, saving 111 turn compared to departing after training.

  7. Except at the start of the game, when the opponent trains soldiers, train the same number of soldiers and gather them at the base located nearest to all of our bases (the center of our bases).

  8. If minimum labor force+1\text{minimum labor force} + 1minimum labor force+1 or more warriors gather at the opponent's stronghold, or if 333 or more warriors move together from the opponent's HQ, the "defense process" activates.

    • Defense process: the defense process collectively refers to the following procedure. It is the work of blocking the opponent's invasion with the minimum number of personnel, and the detailed operation is as follows.

      If minimum labor force+1\text{minimum labor force} + 1minimum labor force+1 troops are present at the opponent's stronghold, dispatch 111 warrior to the stronghold nearest it, and if troops gather beyond that, dispatch the minimum troops needed to block, per the "combat simulation," to the base nearest that base. If they actually attack with troops gathered at their base, run the "movement prediction simulation." At this point, if my base nearest the enemy base where the defending troops have gathered is not included in the simulation results, move the defending personnel to the base among those included that is farthest from the HQ. (Depending on the number of attacking personnel, rerun the combat simulation and dispatch as many as needed.) This process is continuously recalculated and repeated according to the opponent's movements.

      Also, if 333 or more warriors move together from the opponent's HQ, dispatch defending personnel from the rally point — as many as the "combat process" result — to whichever of my bases included in the "movement simulation" results is farthest from the HQ. However, dispatch at the most optimal timing, taking into account my movement distance, the opponent's movement time, and resources (meaning: don't have them dispatched in advance). This lets us narrow the movement simulation results as much as possible.

      If a stronghold where defending personnel had been dispatched drops out of the "movement simulation" results, move the defending personnel to whichever of my strongholds in the simulation is farthest from the HQ (as many as the value resulting from the combat simulation).

      This defense process automatically terminates when its activation conditions are no longer met, and the remaining defensive personnel are moved back to the central base among our bases. At this point, if the opponent moves troops from base to base, cases arise that the above algorithm alone cannot handle; to solve this, move defending personnel to our stronghold positioned in the same direction as the enemy soldiers' movement direction, according to the "combat simulation" results.

    • What the combat simulation is: a calculation, synthesizing both sides' HQ levels and base levels, of whether the HQ or base in question can be destroyed as turns pass, based on warrior health and the attack power of the HQ and bases.

    • Movement prediction simulation: a simulation that, based on the game's movement rules, assumes the opponent moves in the manner that minimizes the sum of Euclidean distances and thereby tracks their destination. It assumes my HQ and strongholds and the opponent's strongholds as candidate destinations and predicts the actual destination by elimination according to the opponent's movement.

  9. Repeat the above steps 1–8, and accumulate leftover money continuously rather than wasting it. In this process, raise the HQ's level the moment enough leftover money to do so has accumulated (however, if that HQ upgrade is judged to interfere with steps 1–8 that must be observed on that turn, do not carry out the upgrade on that turn).

The prompt we wrote

Back around July 3–4 we had talked with the team about the following 2 elements:

  1. The importance of optimizing turn count
  2. The order of game commands

We did realize these 222 were important elements, but at the time we were preoccupied with revising the algorithm right then and there, and we left them alone because we didn't know how to actually make use of them. However, once we redesigned the strategy, their importance gradually became visible. It was the fruit of a great deal of failure and experience.

Where we properly applied this was in the process of redesigning the movement process. In the newly designed movement process, taking into account that minimum-Euclidean-sum movement and minimum-tile movement can differ as discussed above, we added an algorithm that selects the route that uses turns more efficiently, along with a technique called "switching."

The switching technique is a movement technique discovered by our teammate GGRIM, a technique for moving one tile faster by using our own bases (it's explained in detail in the prompt above). Because of the game's basic command order of build → move → train, this could also be applied when sending troops out from the HQ, so almost all movement could be made 111 or more turns faster.
Our movement process, optimized this way, meant that in matches against any other team, we were at least never behind on early build-up speed.

And so at 3 PM on July 8, we tested this defense strategy for the first time.

The result was an ordinary score, but opening it up revealed a rather interesting pattern.

As you can see, while it beat Diamond-level strategies, by the nature of a defense strategy there were also situations where it couldn't even beat a Bronze-level strategy.

So we hurriedly designed a simple attack strategy to handle those weak opponents, blended it in, fixed the bugs that had shown up, and submitted the resulting strategy at 9 PM — and the result:

We completed a strategy that didn't fall far behind even when facing actual Ruby-level strategies. Of course, there were a fair few matches we lost due to some bugs and errors, so our ranking didn't come out as high as that.

And so, while watching the Pretest standings come in, we quickly fixed problems at the same time, and finally completed our final submission at 9:56 PM. Of course, since we couldn't test it, I don't know whether it was actually fixed… we can only pray.
