Early Design

First Concept of the Raid Algorithm

Submission #54392

Submitted July 2, 21:001

Early in NYPC, after seeing a strategy that stacks up 666 warriors and then immediately marches 555 of them at the opponent's HQ to bring it down or wipe out their forces, we thought: "if defense isn't handled properly, an aggressive strategy will work well in the early game." Then came the thought, "rather than hitting the HQ, wouldn't hitting bases with a somewhat smaller force be effective too?" — and that is how the first raid algorithm was born. At first we simply decided to gather three warriors and invade an enemy base, because three warriors are guaranteed to win against a level-111 base garrisoned by a single enemy warrior and destroy it. But realizing that was not enough, we compared our warriors’ combat power\text{our warriors' combat power}our warriors’ combat power against enemy base+warriors’ combat power\text{enemy base} + \text{warriors' combat power}enemy base+warriors’ combat power and decided to raid with a number of warriors that was certain to win. We simulated the game's combat as-is to compute the minimum number of warriors needed for victory, then added a safety margin of +1+1+1 so the raid would not go wrong.

The algorithm we designed was as follows.

  • Right at the start, build one base on a nearby neutral stronghold and garrison it so it can labor, because increasing the early income is extremely important.
  • Then pick the enemy base closest to our HQ as the raid target, compute the number of warriors needed to break it using the simulation described above, and launch the raid as soon as that many warriors have gathered. On a successful raid, build a base on the spot immediately, leave just one laborer, and return.
  • If there is nothing to raid, surplus warriors exist, and there is money to build a base, move the surplus warriors to empty strongholds and expand.
  • Keep adding warriors whenever money allows so that neither the raid party nor the HQ garrison runs short. Once the simulation says our forces exceed the enemy HQ's combat power, lead everyone to hit the enemy HQ.

We generated the code with Google Gemini. The initial "gather three warriors and attack" algorithm came out of the following conversation.

With just this, the bot never attacked the HQ afterward and produced only draws or losses, so we added separate HQ-attack logic; and since attacking with only three was inefficient, we added the simulation above so that only raids certain to win were carried out.

Introducing a Phase Structure

Submission #60916

Submitted July 3, 17:00

We found the algorithm so far was far too focused on raiding. To fix this we split the game into phases by turn and specified which actions take priority in each phase.

There are 333 phases: before turn 150150150, between turns 150150150 and 180180180, and after turn 180180180.

  • Before turn 150150150 (normal operation)
    Proceeds just like the previous raid algorithm. Designed as the phase for building the first base, then raiding and adding strongholds to widen the economic gap and produce the forces for the final battle. Raid targets were chosen starting with the enemy base closest to our HQ. When a raid party succeeded in this phase, priority was pinned on actually building a base at that spot; once construction finished, only the laborers stayed and the rest returned to the HQ. A raid was judged successful by checking that the enemy base at the designated target was gone and our warriors remained.
  • Turns 150150150 ~ 180180180 (final-battle preparation)
    In this phase we stop expanding and raiding and train forces according to the following priorities.
    1. If forces that raided before turn 150150150 are still on a neutral stronghold, build a base there first.
    2. Train warriors according to base levels and the gold on hand.
  • After turn 180180180 (final battle)
    Send the warriors trained at the HQ toward the enemy HQ in turn, as movement cost allows. Only warriors sitting at the HQ are used for the attack; those at bases stay put so they can keep generating movement cost. Gold left over after movement is spent on producing more warriors.

We also built a safety net for HQ defense.

When enemy forces come within 444 areas of our HQ and their number is judged lethal — higher than our HQ level plus our warrior count — the laborers at our bases gather at the HQ to defend. Also, with a base race in mind, if we judge we can destroy their HQ first, we attack the enemy HQ instead of coming back to defend ours.

HQ upgrades followed this policy.

They proceed when no higher-priority action is pending; before turn 404040, only when there are no neutral strongholds left, we have 555 or more warriors, or we have the upgrade cost plus the cost of 222 more warriors in reserve. After that, when our HQ HP is lower than or equal to the opponent's, or when our warrior count is at least 4×4\times4× the HQ level.

Improving Raid Efficiency

Using version 60916 we found the Pretest results fell broadly into three cases.

  1. Against opponents with no defense algorithm, vulnerable to raids, we snowball quickly and win comfortably.

  2. Against opponents with a defense algorithm, raids keep getting blocked and the snowball rolls the other way.

  3. Against opponents using an outpost, we keep attacking only the outpost and the raid party dies pointlessly.

    [An "outpost" means an algorithm that gathers its forces at a base other than the HQ and fights from there. We were especially vulnerable to algorithms that used the central stronghold as an outpost.]

At this point we debated "should we drop the raid algorithm and go with something new?", but rather than imitating opponents we chose to strengthen our own raid algorithm and play the game at our own pace.

So we split things into 333 cases and tried to improve performance by bypassing defense against opponents with a defense algorithm, and by changing raid-target priorities against opponents using outposts.

Reusing the Raid Party Consecutively

Submission #70126

Submitted July 4, 18:00

Previously, the raiding force returned to the HQ before setting out on the next raid. We thought the turns spent returning were wasteful, so to improve this we made the raid party move straight to the next raid site instead of returning to the HQ.

The conditions for a consecutive raid were designed as follows.

  1. Not currently in emergency-defense state.
  2. Not in base-race all-in state.
  3. Enemy bases still remain.
  4. After a successful raid, enough warriors remain to raid again. If only one remains, that warrior waits at the raided spot as the standby who will build the base; if 222 or more remain, the next raid proceeds.

Since raids now continue back to back, we changed it so that once a raid succeeds — the enemy base at the target is gone and the party has been assigned a new target — one warrior is sent from the HQ to the raided neutral stronghold to build the base.

After implementing this, we ran 200200200 practice games against each of the algorithms we had built earlier and compared win rates, but found only a marginal change.

Capturing Two Strongholds Early

Submission #70282

Submitted July 4, 18:00

Previously we took just one stronghold before starting the first raid, but in most Pretests we saw opponents use their initial 333 warriors by leaving one at the HQ and sending the other two to two different neutral strongholds, so we checked whether that worked for us too.

Early on, two warriors are sent to the two different neutral strongholds closest to the HQ, and raiding does not start until both bases are built.

Practice games after applying this showed a higher win rate, and we decided to keep it in the final version.

But the hard requirement of taking 222 strongholds before moving on to raiding meant we could do nothing against an opponent who attacked early, so in the final version we changed it to switch automatically to the raid phase once turn 303030 passes.

Adding Warriors After a Failed Raid

Submission #70693

Submitted July 4, 19:00

Against opponents with a defense algorithm, we thought sending the same number of raiders every time was pointless. So we experimented with recording earlier failures and adjusting the headcount for later raids.

First, after a failed raid, later raids get 111 extra warrior each time, capped at 666 extra.

Raid failures were recorded as follows.

  • Raid target tracking
    Remember which enemy base we raided, so the location is stored for judging failure later.
  • Raid failure criterion
    Check whether an enemy base still remains at the target and whether any of our warriors are still fighting on it; if the enemy base remains and none of our warriors do, the raid is judged a failure.

Practice games again showed a higher win rate. But when we ran a Pretest with this algorithm included, performance actually dropped, so we decided not to include it in the final version.

Running Two Raid Parties, Changing Target Selection

Submission #77433

Submitted July 5, 15:00

We then concluded that headcount adjustment is meaningless if the opponent's defense algorithm reacts to the size of our force, and that increasing the number of raid parties might be more effective.

Raid failure was judged the same way as before, but this time we increased the number of raid parties, allowing up to 444 to operate at once. We hoped this would create guerrilla warfare and let us hit wherever the opponent's defenders were absent.

Since several raid parties were running, we also changed how raid targets were chosen.

Initially we targeted the nearest enemy base, but against outpost algorithms the raid party charged the outpost every time and died pointlessly, so we changed it.

The selection rule:

  1. The weakest enemy base (the one needing the fewest raiders)
  2. If there are several, the one farthest from the enemy HQ
  3. If there are still several, the one closest to our HQ

Practice games again showed a higher win rate, just as before. But performance did not recover in the Pretest; we concluded that the algorithms we used as opponents in practice games could not stand in for the results of top-tier matches, and lowered the weight we gave to practice-game results.

We still thought the new target-selection rule would help when applied to the original algorithm, and indeed, changing only the target selection in the original algorithm raised its performance.

Devising a Way Around the Opponent's Defense

Submission #85365

Submitted July 5, 18:00

Next we thought about bypassing the defense itself. If opponents treat a large group of our warriors emerging at once as a threat and prepare to block it, then sending them one at a time, rallying in front, and entering all at once might work.

Unlike before, once a raid target is set, the areas adjacent to it become rally candidates; warriors gather one by one at the rally point and then go in together.

The rally point is chosen as follows.

  1. Put every area adjacent to the target stronghold on the candidate list.
  2. First remove the one adjacent area that lies on the shortest path to the candidates.
  3. Among the remaining candidates, choose the one closest to our HQ.
  4. If the path to that rally point passes through an enemy stronghold, remove that candidate.
  5. If every candidate is removed, choose the shortest-path area that was excluded at the start.

We did it this way because, if the opponent predicts from our movement paths, deliberately taking a non-shortest route and then raiding should slip past them.

Also, if a new enemy base appears partway along the path to the rally point, the raid target is switched to that base — to avoid losing raiders one at a time while they gather.

After applying this algorithm we took 111 loss against the sample AIs, but since the point was to check whether the defense was bypassed, we checked the result in the Pretest.

It turned out the bypass did not work as hoped, so in the end this version was not used in the final stage.

In this way we explored raising the efficiency of raiding itself, and based on the results decided what to keep in the final version and what to drop.

The strategies included in the final version:

  1. Capture 222 strongholds early, then switch to the raid phase
  2. The new target-selection rule
  3. Use of an outpost

In the final stage we concluded that more raid parties or more raiders only raise the risk of a raid if it does not succeed, and so they were excluded.

Defense Algorithm

Early-Rush Defense Algorithm

Submission #76938

Submitted July 5, 14:00

We found that in the Pretest we lost every game to an early 555-warrior rush. We devised a way to prevent this and called it, internally, the Zerg defense algorithm.

Before designing it, we confirmed that the opponent's algorithm was not complicated, and that opponents using this kind of attack all behaved identically in the early game. Since games against Zerg that reached the late game ended in wins, we figured we only needed to hold the early game.

Because the opponent's algorithm was simple, we easily replicated it with GPT 5.5, then used the CLI tool with the replica and our modified algorithm, swapping sides over seeds 111~100100100 for 200200200 games in total, to check whether the win rate improved.

We built the Zerg defense in several versions and picked the one with the best win rate.

V1. Separate 333-area / 222-area defense

Since the early game was the problem, we added a branch that works only during the first 303030 turns, and changed the form of defense by distinguishing 333 areas and 222 areas around the HQ.

  • Within 333 areas
    Keep normal operation, but produce 111 warrior first
  • Within 222 areas
    Treat it as a direct threat to the HQ: pause expansion/raiding/construction, recall the 111 warrior that went out to expand early, and produce 222 more warriors at the HQ.

This raised the win rate against Zerg sharply, but could not block it completely, and new losses against the sample AIs appeared, so further fixes were needed. First we found the code's "within N areas" detection was wrong, and fixed that.

V2. Detection by actual travel distance

Initially we used a shortest-distance value like P.dist, but it was based on coordinate distance and could differ from how many turns it actually takes to arrive. In the revision we treated each area as a graph node and switched detection to the number of edges between them.

With this we won against the sample AIs again, but the Zerg win rate still was not 100%100\%100%, so we made further changes.

V3. Adding a defense-mode state

Initially, an enemy within 222 areas was handled purely as defense, so the defense logic toggled on and off every turn. We changed it so that once defense activates, it stays active until 222 extra defenders have been produced, and returns to normal operation once the threat is gone and the extra production is done. On returning to normal operation, if any of our bases is empty, the minimum laborers are filled in first for the sake of HQ defense.

There was also a bug where Zerg defense triggered after turn 303030, so we locked it out firmly after turn 303030 so late-game attacks would not be disrupted.

General-Purpose Defense Algorithm

Submission #86106

Submitted July 6, 16:00

With the Zerg defense in place we defended effectively against opponents that mass-produce warriors early and attack our HQ, but problems remained.

First, when the opponent attacked a base near our HQ rather than the HQ itself, we took the wrong approach: not defending the base, but defending from the HQ. Second, because the Zerg defense activates whenever forces appear within nnn areas of our HQ, on small maps where the opponent's strategy was to take the center stronghold, we misread it as an early mass attack on our HQ, and the laborers at every surrounding base were ordered to stay locked in the HQ defending instead of earning money.

The second case in particular is quite fatal: the opponent takes several strongholds and earns money fast, while we earn from the HQ alone despite there being no real threat, so once the Zerg defense finally deactivates, the growth gap is huge. Feeling the limits of the Zerg defense, we set out to implement a new defense algorithm.

Also, there were many games against more aggressive opponents that we judged winnable if only the defense held, so we expected a defense algorithm to bring a somewhat higher win rate.

First, a quick definition: among our forces, warriors not laboring at the HQ or at strongholds are called surplus warriors.

The simplest defense algorithm sends these surplus warriors to whichever of our bases or HQ the opponent is attacking. The problem is that if we send them once the attack is already underway, it is probably too late. Most opponents bundle their forces and hit one of our bases or the HQ all at once, so by the time we respond to an attack the base has most likely already been demolished. And since our current algorithm gathers forces at the HQ, the bigger the map, the clearer this limit becomes.

So instead of blindly building a simple defense, we studied the algorithms of high-rated Pretest opponents that had defended well against us, organized what they did, and used it to shore up our strategy.

The pseudocode for the defense algorithm:

Suppose we have strongholds 1,2,3,4,…1, 2, 3, 4, \dots1,2,3,4,… (the HQ is stronghold 111)

  • Several enemy warriors (333 or more) come in to attack
  • list = []
  • Measuring the enemy's path, estimate that our stronghold nnn is the target
    → add stronghold nnn to list
  • Among the strongholds in list, find the stronghold kkk reachable by the shortest distance
    (e.g., distance 333 between strongholds 111 and 222, 555 between 111 and 333, 777 between 222 and 333 — in that case gathering at stronghold 111 is optimal)
  • Send enemy force sent+2\text{enemy force sent} + 2enemy force sent+2 defenders to stronghold kkk

The idea behind this is as follows. Most high-rated opponents' defense algorithms fire when they detect several of our warriors moving somewhere. Taking inspiration from that, we internally remember the paths the enemy has been travelling and, using the fact that they always reach our base or HQ by the shortest path, estimate which of our bases (or the HQ) they will attack.

One of the most important things in defense is reaching the point to be defended on roughly the same turn as the opponent. So, as in the pseudocode, forces are sent ahead to the stronghold judged fastest to reach the likely defense sites.

We do not send every warrior to the defense site; we match the enemy's force size, for two reasons.

First, once the defense is judged sufficient there is no need to send more. Sending every surplus warrior to that one site leaves other sites exposed — that is, if the opponent attacked two places at once, our defense algorithm would fail.

Second, our strategy is to raid (attack) actively. We have to keep attacking enemy bases as soon as forces gather, and pouring every surplus warrior into defense would stop us attacking. We judged this would be a serious problem for our strategy, so capping the number of defenders was essential.

We also added logic called path-overlap filtering. When a stronghold is predicted from the enemy's path, if reaching that stronghold necessarily requires passing through another of our strongholds, that stronghold is removed. It runs in situations like this:

Let AAA be the enemy's starting point and BBB and B′B'B′ two of our strongholds. If enemy forces leaving AAA must pass through stronghold BBB to reach B′B'B′ by the shortest distance, B′B'B′ is removed from the defense candidates. This runs when we look for stronghold kkk in the algorithm above. We expected it to solve the problem of kkk being pulled too far toward our own side, pushing the timing back and making the defense late.

We designed this algorithm ourselves through discussion, and asked the Gemini AI to write the overall code for the implementation. Errors that came up afterward we fixed ourselves by going through replays of many games.

Patching the Defense Algorithm

Submission #86208

Submitted July 6, 16:00

While pitting the defense algorithm above against our original algorithm we found a serious problem with path-overlap filtering: when the opponent attacked our HQ directly, the logic fell apart and blocked nothing at all.

Since this logic was originally built in the hope of reacting a little faster to the opponent's attack tempo, we judged that removing it would cause no great harm, and fixed the problem by deleting the path-overlap filtering algorithm.

Final Submission Stage

Submission #89004

Submitted July 6, 22:00

For the final submission we combined only the experiments that had shown valid results.

The algorithm has 444 broad stages.

  1. Opening
    Secure 222 of our bases, or until turn 303030
  2. Normal operation (before turn 150150150)
    Expansion, defense, raiding and outpost rallying in parallel
  3. Final-battle preparation (the final attack turn is decided dynamically after turn 150150150)
    Stop new raids and base construction
    Leave the laborers and rally at the outpost
    Produce/upgrade with a target of 353535 warriors at the HQ
  4. Final attack
    Decide the attack turn by map size, and send the forces from the outpost and the HQ at the enemy HQ within the movement cost available

The final version also merged the defense algorithm and the raid algorithm built earlier.

  1. Defense priority
    In the final submission, defense has the highest priority: it is handled before raiding, expansion and late-game preparation, and then the turn ends. It was built by porting the defense from 86106, with Codex's help. Also, if the opponent has 000 bases during the first 555 turns, we treat it as a Zerg situation and postpone base construction until we too have 555 warriors at the HQ.
  2. Raid procedure
    Raid targets are chosen by the target-selection rule defined earlier, and raids run only between turn 404040 and turn 150150150, only when warriors and gold are to spare, with at most two parties.
  3. Outpost operation
    With defense and raiding running simultaneously, having everything move from the HQ became inefficient, so we decided to operate an outpost. During the opening, the HQ serves as the outpost; afterward, each turn we pick, among the strongholds we hold, the one closest to the map center and closer to our HQ as the outpost. The outpost's roles:
    • Departure point for raid parties
    • Standby point for defenders
    • Rally point for the final attack force
    • Shorter travel times on large maps
  4. Final attack
    Instead of launching at a fixed turn 180180180 as before, the final version decides dynamically, taking into account the time needed to reach the enemy HQ. The earliest attack turn is 165165165 and the default start is 180180180, varying dynamically in between. From turn 150150150 we aim to secure 353535 warriors; the first sortie is decided by whether there is enough movement cost for 50%50\%50% of the gathered force to depart. Only warriors able to arrive before turn 200200200 count as candidates; if 50%50\%50% can be gathered we wait and then sortie, and if arrival before turn 200200200 is impossible we send as many as can make it first. From the point when arrival becomes impossible, production stops and the HQ is upgraded to win on the tiebreak.

Additional Efforts

Below, unrelated to our final submission, are the submissions along the way that failed but into which we put a lot of effort.

Reinforcement Learning

Submissions #80380 etc.

Submitted –

Our original goal was to build algorithms that play quite well and then use them to train an AI with reinforcement learning. We actually wrote RL code and trained against our algorithms, but the results were poor. Most of the trained agents either used only very simple strategies (such as sending many warriors at the enemy HQ early) or acted to their own disadvantage.

To fix this we put in a great deal of specific effort.

Believing reward is the most important part of RL, we tuned the reward finely so the agent would act as close as possible to the ideal behavior we had in mind. If winning the game itself is rewarded heavily early on, the agent only performs early rushes (massing warriors and hitting the enemy HQ) against the random agent and the sample code. It would not even destroy the enemy HQ — its goal was simply to deal 111 damage to it, so after attacking the enemy HQ once it would send the warriors back to our HQ. So early on, rather than rewarding winning, we rewarded money heavily so it would earn a lot: reward proportional to total money earned, reward for reaching empty strongholds, building bases on strongholds, upgrading, and so on.

We also judged that moving too much was bad. Moving between strongholds does not matter much, but when moving toward an enemy stronghold, issuing a single Move command saves money. So we penalized excessive movement to make it move with as few commands as possible.

Even with these rewards, the agent's overall strategy did not change at all (e.g., where it used to just charge, it now built 111 stronghold and then just charged). Suspecting overfitting, we retrained a fresh agent from scratch, but it was not much different. We judged that raising the AI past a certain level in the remaining time would be hard, and that spending the time on algorithms would yield better results than spending more on the AI, so we gave up on RL and put our effort into developing a better algorithm.

Build-Up Algorithm

Submissions #99164, #100059, #100083, #102662, #104287 etc.

Submitted –

Through the Pretests we came to feel that an aggressive strategy (the raid algorithm) was not a good idea. We kept adding strategies, yet our rank kept falling.

The first reason is that, by the nature of this game, attacking enemy bases carries a lot of risk. If the attack on the base fails, the warriors are simply thrown away. Also, since the enemy base attacks our warriors too, against an equal force the enemy always has more survivors. Worse, the raid algorithm gives up building or upgrading bases in order to attack, so a failed assault on a base is a heavy loss.

Second, assuming two players of similar skill, defense is favorable in most situations. To succeed in attacking an enemy base you must bring 111 more warrior than they have. And the base being attacked is usually close to the enemy HQ, so even if we march a large force to the attack point first, they can train more warriors at the HQ and prepare. This is magnified on larger maps: the bigger the map, the more turns it takes to reach the enemy base, and the more forces they can defend with in the meantime.

From these two results we concluded that the raid algorithm has a ceiling unless the opponent's defense algorithm can be fully broken. So we began developing a build-up algorithm: rather than actively attacking enemy bases, slowly take empty strongholds, maximize our labor capacity, and topple the opponent in one blow. We decided to develop the build-up and raid algorithms side by side and use whichever performed better.

We kept the broad frame of the original raid algorithm (different strategies per phase, the central-stronghold selection algorithm) and modified/added the following.

  1. Laborer logic
    Every stronghold must hold as many warriors (hereafter "laborers") as its labor capacity, and if a stronghold has no laborer, sending one there is executed first. This is the top-priority action unless the defense algorithm signals that laborers are needed. Laborers are chosen from the surplus warriors starting with the longest-trained (lowest-numbered), but a laborer already working is not replaced.

  2. Build-up order

    Taking empty strongholds proceeds as follows.

    After laborer assignment, the highest-priority order is defense. Afterword

Kimtaka7

  • Overall it was tremendously fun. I am simply grateful that a contest exists where you pit strategies against other teams, like Kaggle. During the contest I kept thinking about it even in the middle of other work, constantly wondering "how could I make a better strategy?"
  • I have many regrets about myself. First, I regret starting reinforcement learning early on without a proper plan. Having never done RL before, I went in with the complacent thought "this should be enough," and the results were poor. If the RL had properly succeeded we might have achieved a better result, and I feel sorry that my teammates suffered because of me. Next time I get a chance like this I should be more careful and thorough.
  • I think we started developing the build-up algorithm far too late. If we had designed it a bit earlier and moved quickly into development, we could have pitted the build-up algorithm against our raid algorithm and built a strategy that covers each other's weaknesses.
  • This contest reminded me once again how important careful planning is. I do not know how the final result will turn out, but it was a good contest that brought back a lesson I had forgotten.

YePpLe

  • I think the experience of understanding a game's rules, designing an algorithm and building a game agent yourself is not easily come by. Through NYPC I got a great opportunity to build a game bot.
  • The Pretest element fired us up even more. Battling our algorithm against other teams' bots showed us, through the game results, the problems in our algorithm and what we could learn from theirs, and it gave us reason to keep thinking about how to improve, so we could work harder on the contest.
  • I have one regret. After coming up with the raid algorithm first, I think it was good that I shared the idea with my friends, proposed our direction and decided which way to go. But in the process of coming up with various algorithms afterward and putting them in code, things did not run the way I wanted, and I feel I was not much help to the team. I also think it would have been better if I had shared my ideas more actively and broadened my teammates' thinking a little more.

ilmsangte

  • Unlike problems where you solve a fixed problem in a fixed way, fighting other people's algorithms with the one you built made this more enjoyable than other contests. I liked that it was not over once you built something: watching the Pretest results, figuring out our algorithm's strengths from the win logs and its weaknesses from the loss logs, and continually developing it.
  • Also, since the number of Pretests was fixed, there was the fun of choosing which algorithm to submit and see results for each time. I had to keep in mind that the Pretest is not just a place to check my own position but a place to see opponents' strategies — and that, conversely, my strategy is exposed too.
  • I do wonder whether the result would have changed if we had kept our strategy hidden from opponents early on and revealed it late in the contest so they could not counter it. The Pretest is another place where my strategy can be exposed, and I think I gave that too little thought.
