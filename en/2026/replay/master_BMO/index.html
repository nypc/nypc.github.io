Solution

Summary

We proceeded in the order: understanding the sample AIs → Strategy 01 (the strategy of our final submission) → Strategy 02 (failed) → Strategy 03 (failed) → Strategy 04 (failed).

We describe in detail the sample-AI analysis that led to the final submission's strategy, Strategy 01 itself, and the problems and fixes at each step. Strategies 02, 03 and 04 ultimately failed and are unrelated to the final submission, so they are covered only briefly.

Below are the reasons Strategies 02, 03 and 04 failed.

Strategy 02 failed because we tried to add macro behaviors on top of Strategy 01 — extra base construction, HQ upgrades and so on — but that only blunted Strategy 01's aggressiveness and made it perform worse.

Strategy 03 failed because, although we designed it to play defensively, secure a stable gold income and then punish the opponent's openings, we did not define the behaviors in enough detail even though it is a rule-based AI. Some steps did not execute properly and the flow between steps was not smooth. There was not enough time to fix this, so we stopped improving it.

Strategy 04 failed because many other teams showed tendencies similar to our Strategy 02 attack, so we focused purely on defense to counter them — but that made our attacks far too conservative and the timing hard to get right, so we stopped improving it as well.

Strategy 01 had no answer to a mirror of itself, and it lost when the opponent was waiting with more than 555 trained warriors. Strategy 02 was prepared to address this.

Fixing the attack force at 555 is strong against a level-1 base+1 garrisoned warrior\text{level-}1\text{ base} + 1\text{ garrisoned warrior}level-1 base+1 garrisoned warrior composition, but once the opponent upgrades to level 222 we just lose warriors. There was also an attack-route problem: when an enemy base or HQ lay on the attack route, combat broke out somewhere other than the destination. We added a check of the attack path in advance, with a detour when necessary.

Rather than making attacks more conservative in case one fails, the first attack uses 555 and later ones 666, for +1+1+1 damage and spare bodies to shorten the replenishment cycle. Attack destinations are also limited to bases we can reach 222 turns earlier than enemy reinforcements, eliminating the reinforcement variable. After a successful attack we move to and build at the center, gaining fewer attack-movement turns.

As for timing, right after the opponent builds a base they have usually spent all their gold, so attacking then means they cannot train reinforcements and the base can be destroyed.

For additional bases, once we have 3×3\times3× as many bases as the opponent we stop building, keep 666 warriors, and take the timing for the level-222 HQ upgrade.

Based on this strategy we asked Codex to modify the Strategy 01 code.

After the changes, checking the Strategy 01 code and the local-test log files showed that, contrary to our requests, instead of building 222 bases and then gathering forces and watching the situation, it immediately built an extra base at the center and then added yet another stronghold. We could also confirm bugs where the attack-decision logic was so conservative it would not attack even destroyable bases; where, with no stronghold left to build on and no attacks either, gold just piled up; where headcount allocation broke so that 555 never gathered at or near the center; and where, after the opponent destroyed a base, gold went unspent and the warriors stopped at the destroyed base without rebuilding.

So to fix these bugs we requested the following changes.

Using the attack-decision function, compute every turn how many warriors each enemy base/HQ needs, attack starting from the one needing the fewest, and lift the attack headcount cap when necessary to ease the conservatism.

When the enemy attacks, pin down the destination via shortest-route detection logic and send reinforcements equal to the enemy attack force. Half of the defenders come from the attack squad (the smaller half if odd) and half from HQ training. The attack squad's detachment moves when the enemy's remaining movement turns to the destination equal the turns the detachment needs to reinforce.

The rally point must be the frontline base closest to the enemy's bases.

When defending or attacking, always move with the whole force gathered, to reduce the chance of being defeated piecemeal.

In battles against other teams we found a losing case: the opponent trains 444 warriors with the starting 500500500 gold right away and attacks our HQ with 666.

The current code could not yet handle this attack, so to address it we added: when an enemy attack is detected, the destination is our HQ, and our garrison is smaller than the attacking force, then to live to fight another day, gather every warrior except 111 laborer (including laborers at other bases) at the HQ to meet the attack.

So that our gathering forces do not cross paths with the enemy's attackers, we additionally changed the placement of the first 222 bases to avoid the movement path from the enemy HQ to ours, so that HQ attacks are recognized quickly and defenders are not picked off one by one. Since the attack-decision function was still conservative, we changed it so that a level-111 base with 111 garrisoned warrior is always attacked with a full 555-warrior squad.

We found a bug where inaccurate enemy-attack prediction made the defense misfire, so we simplified it: if several enemy warriors are moving together toward one place, station defenders at the base closest to the enemy force.

Spending gold the moment it appears can actually be poison.

Waiting 111 turn costs only 151515 gold even in the case where the opponent builds a base, and it lets us read what they intend. Reading the intent and responding accordingly seems worth far more than 151515 gold.

Moreover, the order I give this turn can be the reason I cannot respond to the order the opponent gives. e.g., I invest 300300300 gold in a base while the opponent orders 555 warriors to attack my base → no way to respond.

The opponent usually saves or spends gold with a purpose, and once the minimum gold for that purpose is there, they spend it right away. So by acting 111 turn later from the start, we are always 111 turn behind the opponent and can see all of their intent for a 151515-gold loss.

The best timing to attack a base

When the opponent has no defenders and has spent all their gold on base construction or an upgrade, unable to react — if the fight (not merely the attack) begins then, it is pure profit. To defend, they must move laborers, so we gain enormously: their gold income (laborers sent as support) + a destroyed base + lost warriors.

For this we must be sure the opponent is building a base. If we leave it to luck and the attack fails, the opponent gains far more. The reason we need certainty is that if we train warriors and send them only after the opponent has built the base, they have more than enough time to prepare and minimize their losses.

The attack must begin the very turn the opponent builds, or combat must break out within 222–333 turns at most, to raise the success rate much. So warriors must be produced in advance and positioned near the expected construction stronghold (which may be an ordinary area, or one of our strongholds near the expected site). It is a strategy we can only use when we are certain they will build there.

The certainty conditions are: "the opponent's estimated gold is comfortably above 150150150, yet they are not training" and "111 warrior is moving toward a buildable stronghold, or is standing on one."

We took these as certainty conditions because they can be read as "I will capture this spot — sent ahead", or "I have saved the gold for a base, so I am moving to build."

Using ordinary areas rather than strongholds

The fork mentioned above (the key to raising attack success — when the defense is very strong, movement cost is paid 222 times, but the attack succeeds 100%100\%100%), or scouting. Most defense logic responds to the number of moving warriors, predicts their target and positions defenders accordingly. So by sending a few warriors to an ordinary area near an enemy base for scouting, we can see how the opponent reacts and infer their defensive strategy.

Priorities for base placement

A defensive strategy places bases close to our HQ so forces can move quickly; an attacking strategy places them where movement toward enemy bases is easy. How should this be decided per situation? Consider map layout, our strategy, the opponent's strategy, and how location importance depends on the number of buildable strongholds.

First, avoid offering the opponent a fork, because if several of our bases are reachable from one area in the same number of turns, defending becomes drastically harder. Also, most opponents open with a macro strategy of building bases early and almost never cross over past the center, so the key is placing bases well near our own bases and near the center.

Tracking the opponent's gold

Base construction, training, supply, and HQ/base upgrades can be confirmed from the results, so that gold is certain. Movement, however, cannot be pinned down.

Movement cost can be estimated by tracking the opponent's moves: assign each enemy warrior a unique ID, −10-10−10 gold when it moves, +10+10+10 gold when the area it stops on is theirs. That gives a simple estimate.

The problem with this estimate is that when a warrior steps on one of our bases en route or runs into our moving forces, combat happens away from its destination, and if it dies we can no longer know its destination, so an exact calculation is impossible. So we decided to estimate movement cost as a maximum and a minimum.

→ This was a method GPT recommended during implementation: if many warriors die midway or have unclear destinations, the gap between max and min grows too wide, and keeping only the top few candidates beam-search style reduces this, so we adopted it.

Combat simulation

ChatGPT helped with the combat-simulation logic. We listed the factors to consider and asked it to compute the combat simulation using only these. The factors are:

  • Target building HP / turret
  • Attacker HP / ID
  • Current defender HP / ID
  • Backup arriving per day
  • Lowest-HP warrior is hit first
  • On ties, the lower ID is hit first
  • Day the building is destroyed
  • Surviving attackers / HP

Later, to speed up computation, we put the combat data into a data.bin file and used that. data.bin contains:

  • Building type
  • Building level
  • Building current HP
  • Attacker HP bucket
  • Defender HP bucket
  • Number of defending warriors
  • Combat day limit
  • Minimum attackers
  • Whether destruction is possible

On LLMs

We wrote our strategies out as md files, then either had ChatGPT produce work instructions for Codex and handed those over, or gave Codex the md file directly and asked it to implement the described behavior.

The problem with this was that, watching the code run, behaviors we never wanted crept in, and behaviors clearly laid out in the md file were sometimes not executed.

Early on we found the problems ourselves, issued fix instructions one by one until it behaved as we wanted, and then checked whether it had improved. But for things we could not verify by eye — gold-prediction logic, the combat simulation, or complex attack/defense logic — it was hard to tell for sure whether they worked through local tests or battles against the sample AIs. So near the end of the contest we gave the md file and the written code to GPT or Codex and asked, "Does this code properly reflect the md file?" — and every time, the answer was "this is missing" or "that is not done."

Then ChatGPT and Codex always add, "if you instruct Codex like this," or "if you fix it like this, it will apply properly." Following those solutions with Codex, Codex would leave a final report in the format our instructions prescribed saying everything was done — yet it still did not work, which was extremely frustrating. This seems to have been the single biggest reason only the simple early Strategy 01 was improved to good results while Strategies 02, 03 and 04 failed.

Was this a problem of not knowing how to handle AI, since we do not use it much normally? Would it have been fine if we had split the md file's strategy into pieces, implemented them one at a time, and then wired the behaviors together? I still do not know the answer.

We had Codex read the md file summarizing the desired strategy and algorithms directly, or requested code changes with prompts based on its contents, but — perhaps due to code optimization or to avoid TLE — computation-heavy or complex logic often was not applied on the first request, and Codex said nothing about it.

To prevent the same problem we added emphasis with the word "unconditionally" frequently, and added a final step after each change where it compares the md file or prompt with the modified code and reports or re-fixes anything missing. This did not eliminate the problem, but we confirmed it improved from frequently missing 555–666 items to occasionally missing 111–222.
