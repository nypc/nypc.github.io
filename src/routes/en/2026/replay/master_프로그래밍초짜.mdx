Written by: Kera

Back in high school, I — the person writing this — once took on NYPC. That was right around the time I was first
becoming interested in problem solving, and what left a strong impression on me, unlike other contests, was that the
problems genuinely mixed in elements from all sorts of games, and that the format asked you to find your own optimized
answer among many possible ones. I couldn't really participate properly at the time, since my skills were lacking and I
had to juggle schoolwork, but I still have memories of solving and discussing problems together with my high school
friends, and I even attended last year's $10$th anniversary offline event — so this contest has always meant a lot to me.

Time passed, I graduated from high school, and I thought my connection with NYPC was over. But then I heard that the
NYPC University Code Battle, which had felt like a trial run last year, was being launched officially under the name
NYPC Master Track — so I resolved that this time I would take part properly.
And so I decided to take it on together with bananasoyami and GGRIM. Both teammates have excellent mathematical
reasoning and are skilled at strategic design, so I felt very reassured.

After registering, we played the mushroom game ourselves for a bit, talked it over, and discussed a little about how we
should approach things once the actual qualification round began. Then we spent the time until the qualification round
on final exams and personal activities. Time passed, and the day the qualification round began arrived.

On June 29, when I read the problem that had just been made public, two thoughts came into my head: "this is quite
complex" and "this is going to be quite fun."

Reading through the game rulebook calmly, it looked like it was built on top of "Civilization" and "StarCraft." But
between the $200$-turn limit, the order of actions, and the detailed movement rules and battlefield generation rules —
the fact that it explained even the basic parts of the game in such depth gave me a feeling that this was going to get
fairly complicated.

So, in order to understand the rules that weren't clicking for me and to work out a strategy, I first built a simulator
I could play myself.
In the case of the mushroom game — the practice game — you could play it in the simulator by default, which made
understanding the rules and devising strategy really convenient. However, right after building it myself, what I felt
was: "ah, now I see why they didn't make one for us this time."

The more soldiers there were each turn, and the more complex the operation I wanted to carry out, the more commands I
actually had to issue — and since this had to go on for close to $200$ turns, it was obvious that playing a single game
properly would take an enormous amount of time.

So I gave up on devising strategy by playing it myself, and instead started by looking at the sample code they provided.
The sample code simply had logic that sent $3$ warriors marching forward right at the start, and beyond learning how to
write the code itself, it didn't look like there was anything to gain from it.

The next day, June 30, was the day I decided I should lay the groundwork for the actual working environment going
forward, based on the rules and sample code I had checked the day before.
The previous day I had used an LLM to write a few pieces of code that came to mind, but it didn't produce results as
satisfying as I'd expected.
So, to fix that, I thought hard about more efficient ways of instructing it and of writing the code.

(As for the LLM models used, we worked on the basis of Anthropic's Claude, mainly using Sonnet and Fable.)

Here's what I realized:

1. Leaving the entire code-writing process to the LLM is not a good approach. So we need to structure things well enough
   that we can read, at least roughly at the code level, how the actual logic works.
2. For the strategy design process, you definitely should not rely on the LLM's help. At first we discussed with it what
   the best strategy would be based on the rules, but as time went on I realized that the strategies we thought through
   and built ourselves were far more efficient and far better.
3. When writing prompts, organize what you want implemented as cleanly as possible before handing it over. In
   particular, numbering your requirements one by one (item 1 ~, item 2 ~) rather than writing in conversational prose
   produces much better results.

Based on what we'd realized, we split the code's structure into a variable declaration section, a section that reads
basic server information, and the `decide()` function where the actual strategy lives — and once we wrote the code that
way, we were thankfully able to implement the strategy we wanted.

And so on July 2, with submission 46329, we managed to produce a strategy that reached Gold tier[^1] for the first time.
To describe it a little more precisely, the basic skeleton of this strategy was as follows.

1. Capture the center point of the map and its surroundings as the top priority, and after that simply capture the
   strongholds near our HQ one by one.
2. Once capturing is finished, dispatch 5 warriors at a time to enemy strongholds to destroy them, build a stronghold on
   that spot, and expand one by one.
3. Level up the HQ to level 3 along the way, and after that watch the opponent and stay $+1$ above them.

There were minor HQ defense routines and other details mixed in, but overall this was the concept of our first strategy.

However, even after going through numerous error fixes and logic revisions via matches against the sample AI, fighting
actual opponents in the Pretest turned out to be no easy matter. The problems I wrote down in my notes at the
time were as follows.

The initial implementation of the all-out rush strategy was as follows.

1. Of the first 3 warriors, send 1 to the stronghold nearest our HQ, and have the remaining 1 take the base on our side
   that is nearest the map's central stronghold.

   <Figure src="/2026/replay/master_프로그래밍초짜/images/p06_X22.png" caption="(Start of the build-up on side A in the image above)" />

3. After that, train troops and gather them at the rally point; once 5 or more warriors beyond the minimum labor force

   The key point is that, unlike before, we don't build a stronghold — we immediately use those troops to press on and
   invade the enemy's strongholds.
4. Once all strongholds are captured, gather troops after that and march them into the enemy HQ to finish the game.
5. Throughout this entire process, as long as the HQ is not in danger, the minimum labor force for each base and the HQ
   must always remain in place.

After that we fought actual opponents and went through various strategic revisions.

First, the biggest problem was the number of strongholds built in the all-out rush strategy. The initial all-out rush
strategy I described above builds only $2$ strongholds.
As a result, as soon as the map got even a little larger, the efficiency of attacks starting from those strongholds
dropped sharply, and we simply could not win against an opponent who just built up.

So we simply set the number of bases to build to $(\text{number of buildable strongholds on my side} / 3)$, which
produced better results than before… but depending on the situation it was still sometimes too few or too many, so
problems remained.

And although we went through many revisions — calculation formulas, adding logic that decides based on the shape of the
map, and so on — in the end we never fully solved it.

Since we still had no optimization for base construction or movement based on the shape of the map, we also confirmed
that when we ran into a similar all-out rush strategy, we would lose to teams that had optimized better than us.
So in the early hours of July 7, to fix this, we added logic that identifies when the opponent is running an all-out
rush and commits to defense.
The conditions for identifying an opponent as running an all-out rush are as follows.

1. The opponent maintains $2$ bases for $20$ or more turns.
2. After that, they do not build or upgrade bases and spend all their money on training warriors.

And the process carried out once an all-out rush is identified is as follows.

1. Terminate all attack processes being carried out.
2. Always maintain $2$ bases, and if one is destroyed, rebuild it at the buildable location nearest the HQ.
3. Always keep the total warrior count at $7$ or more.
4. For every base, if an enemy warrior comes within $4$ tiles, add it to the "watch" list.
5. If, for a base on the "watch" list, an enemy warrior is detected within $3$ tiles on the next turn, send $3$ warriors
   to that base.
6. If a base is under direct attack, keep sending warriors continuously.
7. If a base is removed from the "watch" list, gather all available soldiers at that location (warriors beyond the
   minimum force) back to the HQ.
8. If enough money accumulates to upgrade the HQ, upgrade it.

Through the above process we were able to win most matchups against all-out rush strategies. In the 10 AM simulation on
July 7, we confirmed that when attacking, if the opponent had a level $2$ HQ, we could not break through even by
attacking with $5$ warriors. So we revised the code that sent a base of $5$ to send
$5+2\times(\text{opponent's level}-1)$ instead. Using this code we were able to put up our best result.

After the all-out rush strategy, the biggest upgrade was building a system that tracks the opponent's movement. The idea
started from the basic fact that movement paths are predetermined. Namely, that movement from a given starting point to
a destination always follows the path of minimum Euclidean sum.

But while trying to understand the battlefield generation rules in order to understand these movement rules more
precisely, I realized one extremely important fact.
Namely, that moving by minimum Euclidean sum does not necessarily guarantee the minimum number of tiles.
In particular, I learned that in the method of constructing the map using Delaunay triangulation, the large polygons
that form on the outside create many routes that take few tiles but have a long Euclidean distance.
I was able to properly grasp this thanks to my teammates GGRIM and bananasoyami, who have strong mathematical reasoning.

In any case, on the basis of this movement scheme, we built logic that predicts enemy troop movement: assuming the
opponent is moving toward their own or our HQ and strongholds, it investigates every possible minimum-Euclidean-sum path
and then eliminates candidates one by one as they deviate from those paths.
It was basically put together by blending various exhaustive search algorithms including BFS, and thankfully it didn't
hit the time limit.

Another one of the big upgrades was building a combat simulation algorithm. To solve a problem that had been chronic
even in the strategies before the all-out rush — attacking and continually losing troops even when we couldn't actually
destroy the enemy's bases and HQ — we needed a simulation that calculated when we could actually destroy an enemy base.

So we built a simulation that, according to the given combat rules, calculates the actual minimum number of troops
needed to destroy a base given the base's level and defending troops, taking both our HQ level and the opponent's into
account.

First, that this game is nothing more than rock-paper-scissors. The $3$ most fundamental strategies that govern this

So the all-out rush strategy had a hard time beating defense strategies, whereas the build-up strategy we implemented

After realizing this, we had $2$ options.

2. Build an entirely new strategy that appropriately mixes defense and build-up.

And so, with our sleep patterns already thoroughly wrecked from running the contest, we pulled another all-nighter.

The foundation of the new strategy was to mix defense and build-up appropriately. The reason lay in the nature of
defense strategies.
A defense strategy collectively refers to making your bases unbreakable by the opponent's attacks, and we discovered
that by exploiting the fact that the attacker normally spends more time moving, it isn't too late to respond after
watching the opponent's movements.

In other words, defense is relatively lower-difficulty than attacking.
(Of course, with more advanced strategies this changes once you use things like fake movement… but we set that aside for
now.)

So we set the basic concept as a strategy that defends against all of the opponent's attacks while gradually growing our
own bases to aim for a health win, and carried out the detailed implementation as follows.

<div style={{ padding: 8, border: "1px solid" }}>

1. The basic action principle is to prioritize building bases starting from the buildable stronghold area nearest our
   HQ. All soldier dispatches happen when a base can be built as soon as they arrive at the construction area.
2. The game's basic premise is that every stronghold and the HQ always has warriors stationed equal to the minimum labor
   force.
3. All movement basically moves along the route that minimizes the number of "tiles" between origin and destination.
   However, since by the movement rules movement proceeds in the direction that minimizes the sum of Euclidean
   distances, if the minimum-tile movement differs from the path minimizing the Euclidean distance sum, then split the
   destination into $2$, $3$… parts if necessary so that movement always takes the minimum number of tiles. However,
   split the destination as few times as possible.
4. If there are multiple routes taking the minimum number of tiles, preferentially choose the route that passes through
   one of our bases along the way where possible.
5. If, once a warrior's route to its destination has been decided by rules 3 and 4, the route passes through one of our
   strongholds, use the "switching" technique to move.
   - Switching: the switching technique is a strategy for moving faster by using another base that lies between the
     destination and the starting point.

     The way to execute it: if the warrior's location is the tile immediately before the base it passes through, then at
     the same time as the warrior moves into that base, move $1$ warrior that was in the base (one that was filling the
     minimum labor force, etc.) onward along the route the original warrior was taking. This makes it possible to gain
     $1$ turn.

     That is, when a situation arises where the switching technique can be used, note that the warrior's movement should
     target the intermediate stronghold rather than aiming at the destination from the start.
6. When training and dispatching warriors from the HQ, just as with the switching technique, move the personnel already
   there and train at the same time, saving $1$ turn compared to departing after training.
7. Except at the start of the game, when the opponent trains soldiers, train the same number of soldiers and gather them
   at the base located nearest to all of our bases (the center of our bases).
8. If $\text{minimum labor force} + 1$ or more warriors gather at the opponent's stronghold, or if $3$ or more warriors
   move together from the opponent's HQ, the "defense process" activates.
   - Defense process: the defense process collectively refers to the following procedure. It is the work of blocking the
     opponent's invasion with the minimum number of personnel, and the detailed operation is as follows.

     If $\text{minimum labor force} + 1$ troops are present at the opponent's stronghold, dispatch $1$ warrior to the
     stronghold nearest it, and if troops gather beyond that, dispatch the minimum troops needed to block, per the
     "combat simulation," to the base nearest that base. If they actually attack with troops gathered at their base, run
     the "movement prediction simulation."
     At this point, if my base nearest the enemy base where the defending troops have gathered is not included in the
     simulation results, move the defending personnel to the base among those included that is farthest from the HQ.
     (Depending on the number of attacking personnel, rerun the combat simulation and dispatch as many as needed.)
     This process is continuously recalculated and repeated according to the opponent's movements.

     Also, if $3$ or more warriors move together from the opponent's HQ, dispatch defending personnel from the rally
     point — as many as the "combat process" result — to whichever of my bases included in the "movement simulation"
     results is farthest from the HQ. However, dispatch at the most optimal timing, taking into account my movement
     distance, the opponent's movement time, and resources (meaning: don't have them dispatched in advance). This lets
     us narrow the movement simulation results as much as possible.

     If a stronghold where defending personnel had been dispatched drops out of the "movement simulation" results, move
     the defending personnel to whichever of my strongholds in the simulation is farthest from the HQ (as many as the
     value resulting from the combat simulation).

     This defense process automatically terminates when its activation conditions are no longer met, and the remaining
     defensive personnel are moved back to the central base among our bases.
     At this point, if the opponent moves troops from base to base, cases arise that the above algorithm alone cannot
     handle; to solve this, move defending personnel to our stronghold positioned in the same direction as the enemy
     soldiers' movement direction, according to the "combat simulation" results.
   - What the combat simulation is: a calculation, synthesizing both sides' HQ levels and base levels, of whether the HQ
     or base in question can be destroyed as turns pass, based on warrior health and the attack power of the HQ and
     bases.
   - Movement prediction simulation: a simulation that, based on the game's movement rules, assumes the opponent moves
     in the manner that minimizes the sum of Euclidean distances and thereby tracks their destination. It assumes my HQ
     and strongholds and the opponent's strongholds as candidate destinations and predicts the actual destination by
     elimination according to the opponent's movement.
9. Repeat the above steps 1–8, and accumulate leftover money continuously rather than wasting it. In this process, raise
   the HQ's level the moment enough leftover money to do so has accumulated (however, if that HQ upgrade is judged to
   interfere with steps 1–8 that must be observed on that turn, do not carry out the upgrade on that turn).

Back around July 3–4 we had talked with the team about the following 2 elements:

1. The importance of optimizing turn count
2. The order of game commands

We did realize these $2$ were important elements, but at the time we were preoccupied with revising the algorithm right

However, once we redesigned the strategy, their importance gradually became visible. It was the fruit of a great deal of

Where we properly applied this was in the process of redesigning the movement process.
In the newly designed movement process, taking into account that minimum-Euclidean-sum movement and minimum-tile
movement can differ as discussed above, we added an algorithm that selects the route that uses turns more efficiently,
along with a technique called "switching."

The switching technique is a movement technique discovered by our teammate GGRIM, a technique for moving one tile faster
by using our own bases (it's explained in detail in the prompt above).
Because of the game's basic command order of build → move → train, this could also be applied when sending troops out
from the HQ, so almost all movement could be made $1$ or more turns faster.\
Our movement process, optimized this way, meant that in matches against any other team, we were at least never behind on
early build-up speed.

## GGRIM.

It was a shame to be short on time, to the point of thinking "what if I had focused on NYPC a bit earlier?"
The Pretest, available only $3$ times a day, was the one moment that made me nervous, and also a moment I
looked forward to.
Even though my head was filled with the thought of whether the strategy we'd built would really work, in one corner
there was also the expectation that with this strategy, surely we could achieve a high ranking.

In fact, as someone without all that much coding experience, it was hard to achieve good results in the existing NYPC,
and I was someone who felt the pressure before participating.
That's why NYPC: Master Track, where you can actively use an LLM and deploy the strategy you want, became an opportunity
that couldn't have been better.
Setting aside its being a good opportunity, just staying up all night discussing and taking on a single goal together
with friends was itself an enjoyable experience.

With this being Master Track's first year, I don't know how next year or the year after will turn out, but this was a
contest that made me think I'd want to participate any time, as long as I can keep participating in this way.
Thank you all for your hard work.
