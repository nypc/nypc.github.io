# Solution

## 6/29

Right after the qualification round began, since our understanding of NEXT NATION was low, we focused on analyzing the core rules of the game. After finishing that analysis, each of us fed the core rules into our own LLM and completed our preparations in earnest. Believing that more diverse strategies are a clear advantage, we decided each member would cultivate a strategy of their own. We tried a variety: an economy strategy focused on the accumulated gold difference; a growth strategy that seizes the surrounding strongholds, focuses on building upgrades, and overwhelms the opponent with the combat-power gap that comes from HQ level; a rush strategy that produces warriors and attacks immediately without building bases; and more.

Since resource efficiency is critical in the RTS genre, we analyzed gold efficiency — for instance the turns needed for a base to turn a profit after construction. The difference in the number of strongholds held directly becomes the difference in accumulated gold. Because the battlefield generation rules make the number of strongholds odd, once all of them are taken, the difference in stronghold count comes down to who holds the exact center. From this we grasped the importance of the central stronghold. We also analyzed the advantage of defense: thanks to the building, a relatively small number of warriors can defend rather than needing equal headcount. A successful defense with fewer warriors can be seen as a relative gold gain. The Pretest results let us confirm the validity of each strategy and which defense strategies countered them successfully, and showed us the direction the top teams' strategies were taking. We could see that to cover the cost of building or upgrading a base, no forces are reinforced for many turns, and that a base is vulnerable to attack right after an upgrade. Exploiting this, we could devise a strategy of aggressively destroying bases around turn $23.07$, the break-even point after construction. We also struggled with resolving CLE, WA and TLE.

## 6/30

Development began in earnest. Referring to the first day's Pretest logs, each of us reinforced our strategy and worked toward the first goal: $8$ wins against the Nexon bots. Some chose a strategy of taking as many strongholds as possible in the early turns to maintain economic wealth; others chose to seize the opponent's bases to widen the per-turn gold production gap. Continuously analyzing logs from simulations against the Nexon bots, we confirmed that a stronghold with one building and one warrior stationed on it can be taken with three warriors. We thought this could be applied to the existing problem of warriors charging one at a time into strongholds they could not win, and decided to use it aggressively. There was also the problem that we were very vulnerable to the Nexon bot strategy of massing warriors and marching straight at the HQ. So we tried building a systematic defense strategy, and as a result the team's first $8$-win bot was born.

Since there are many strongholds that could become targets for the opponent, we focused on a fluid defense that could respond immediately, timed to the arrival turn, based on the headcount of the enemy's combat warriors excluding their laborers. We rebuilt the formulas from the HP and attack values of buildings and warriors stated in the rules, quantified them with an LLM with reuse in mind, and provided an Excel file computing each efficiency, to serve as the foundation for the attack and defense logic we would improve later.

### Submission #40655

Submitted July 1, 1 PM

Internal testing showed the economy bot had a problem: when attacking, it failed to notice enemy warriors joining and kept fighting battles it could not win. It attacked to gain money, but failing to see the enemy reinforce and losing our warriors only brought losses. To fix this, every turn we add the measured combat power of the areas adjacent to the enemy stronghold and refresh the predicted outcome. The Pretest result was $21$st of $557$ teams.

But new problems always followed. To counter rush strategies, we intended that an enemy approaching within $4$ areas of our HQ, or within $2$ areas of a stronghold, be treated as a threat to that building and that defenders join the HQ defense — but this backfired and handed our strongholds over without any resistance. Analyzing the logs with an LLM, we analyzed why these orders were being issued and tried to resolve the cause. It turned out the HQ alert's importance outweighed the stronghold alert, so we changed the alert method and began devising logic that recomputes the defenders' rally point every turn and defends expansions to the extent that they can still reach the HQ on the same turn as the enemy group.

### Submission #49903

Submitted July 2, 12 PM

We reinterpreted the strategy of building $2$ strongholds early, gathering $5$ warriors, then attacking. While attackers were on the move and had not yet reached the target, the target was refreshed every turn and they turned back. Since the criterion for starting an attack after rallying was not established, some warriors started attacking and were defeated piecemeal. Also, our early forces lagged behind the opponent's. The buffering meant to prevent WA was backfiring.

To fix this, the target is fixed once chosen, preventing the turn-back problem, and the buffering was relaxed to secure early warrior production speed. Through the LLM we examined the judgment on every turn using "wrong judgment by turn" as the criterion, and corrected WA, compile errors and unintended behavior via simulation. Combining the importance of the central stronghold confirmed earlier with the monetary gain available from defending, we tried keeping defenders to a minimum and concentrating forces on one target to pressure the enemy HQ

Judging that code changes through the LLM can cause cracks, and to minimize damage from the LLM's characteristic optimism, we verified the facts with a reproduction test after every change. The result was the team's best rank, $15$th.

As the number of bots imitating strategies our bots were weak against grew, the quality of internal test results kept rising, and it became a good environment for confirming strengths and weaknesses. The Pretest could not be attempted often enough to test four people's strategies, so we began relying on internal testing. Repeating the cycle of analyzing logs and improving, we realized something: having the AI write the code first and then patching it through log analysis can produce a reasonably capable model, but repeated, it becomes unmaintainable and increasingly likely to hurt further improvement. To address this, we conceptually split the logic into systems and built a chain of command, so that later improvements could be maintained efficiently — the idea of an object-oriented controller — and began developing through harness engineering.

### Submission #58894

### Submission #77166

# Afterword

There was something all the team members felt in common. For all of us, taking an interest in the game, building RTS strategies, and analyzing each other's strategies through the objective data of logs was a kind of fun hard to find in other competitions. The biggest regret was our mistaken contest strategy during the contest. Pretest submissions were limited, and the number was insufficient for $4$ people to get real evaluations of each strategy.

Given another chance, rather than four people building bots with four different strategies, we would limit ourselves to about two strategies and raise their completeness by reproducing the opponents they proved weak against.

The hard part was the stream of unexpected variables: strengthening the defense logic broke the attack logic, running the gold tight set off chains of WA, and so on. The Galápagos syndrome caused by the team's internal testing was the biggest regret. Perhaps if the number of strategies available for internal testing had been more diverse, this would not have happened; the time invested without realizing Galápagos syndrome was setting in felt wasted, and watching results that had been going so well collapse was crushing. The Galápagos effect hit hardest at the very end of the contest, and the regret that we could not produce our best result lingers.

Working many hours with LLMs, we felt their limits acutely. We thought we had been specific, but LLMs seemed to need even more specific requests. Things got implemented in directions other than intended, and abstract requests with high reasoning difficulty were hard to implement. This made us think again about how important the user's skill is in using an LLM. Another problem: even when we asked the LLM to optimize, or to delete duplicated or conflicting code, in the end it was the user who had to find it. When asked for new logic, it would overwrite rather than change the existing logic.

We felt the LLMs' drawbacks strongly, but at the same time felt their usefulness. Even without confidence in your coding skill, repeated revision eventually produces something close to what you asked for — in that sense, we felt the two-sidedness of LLMs.
